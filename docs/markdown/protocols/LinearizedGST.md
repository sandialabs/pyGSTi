# Linearized Gate Set Tomography with Mid-Circuit Measurements

Standard gate set tomography fits a dense process matrix to every gate, which limits it to one or two qubits. **Linearized GST** ([Miller et al., arXiv:2605.11158](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11158)) instead learns the rates of a *sparse error model*: a polynomial number of elementary error generators (coherent $H_P$ and Pauli-stochastic $S_P$ errors) associated with each gate of a processor, including crosstalk terms.  Because it only uses shallow Clifford circuits, the dependence of every measured Pauli expectation value on the error rates can be linearized, and the rates are recovered by solving a linear system whose coefficients are computed efficiently with pyGSTi's error generator propagation machinery (which requires `stim`).

In this tutorial we walk through a complete linearized-GST analysis of a simulated 3-qubit processor whose gate set includes a **mid-circuit measurement** (MCM).  MCMs are modelled with the virtual-qubit "gadget" of [Wysocki et al., arXiv:2602.03938](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03938), and their errors are reported as the physically interpretable *first-order MCM-gauge-invariant* (FOMGI) quantities introduced there: pure readout error, incomplete collapse ("weakness"), rotations of the measurement axis, and so on.  We will

1. specify a sparse H+S error model for gates, SPAM and the MCM,
2. sample shallow random circuits containing MCMs,
3. build the *design matrix* that linearly relates error rates to observable shifts,
4. simulate data (both without and with shot noise),
5. estimate the error rates and compare them to the truth, and
6. interpret the MCM's estimated error rates.

## 1. Background

### The linear approximation

A noisy circuit layer $\ell$ is modelled as the ideal layer $\mathcal{U}_\ell$ followed by an error channel generated by a Lindbladian, $\tilde{\mathcal{U}}_\ell = e^{\mathcal{L}_\ell}\,\mathcal{U}_\ell$.  The Lindbladian of a layer is the sum of the Lindbladians associated with the gates $g$ in that layer,

$$\mathcal{L}_\ell = \sum_{g\in\ell} \mathcal{L}_g, \qquad \mathcal{L}_g = \sum_{P} h^{(g)}_P\, H_P + \sum_{P} s^{(g)}_P\, S_P,$$

where $H_P[\rho] = -i[P, \rho]$ generates a coherent rotation about the Pauli axis $P$ (a rate $h$ corresponds to a rotation angle of $2h$) and $S_P[\rho] = P\rho P - \rho$ generates a stochastic Pauli error (a rate $s$ corresponds to $P$ being applied with probability $\approx s$).  A gate may cause errors on *any* qubits, not just its targets, which is how crosstalk is modelled.  The sets of Paulis $P$ that appear for each gate form the *ansatz*; their rates $\vec{\epsilon} = (h^{(g)}_P, s^{(g)}_P)$ are the unknowns.

Conjugation by a Clifford maps elementary error generators to elementary error generators (up to a sign), so every gate's error generator can be pushed to the end of the circuit.  To first order in the rates, the change of the expectation value of a Pauli observable $Q$ at the end of a circuit $C$ is linear in $\vec\epsilon$,

$$\Delta\langle Q\rangle_C \equiv \langle Q\rangle_C^{\rm noisy} - \langle Q\rangle_C^{\rm ideal} = \vec{s}_{Q,C}\cdot\vec{\epsilon},$$

and the sensitivity vectors $\vec{s}_{Q,C}$ can be computed with stabilizer arithmetic.  Stacking them over many circuits and observables gives the **design matrix** $D$ with $\overrightarrow{\Delta\langle Q\rangle} = D\vec\epsilon$.  For a stabilizer state, an observable with ideal expectation $0$ is first-order sensitive only to $H$ errors and one with ideal expectation $\pm 1$ only to $S$ errors, so the system decouples: Hamiltonian rates are estimated by pseudo-inversion, $\vec\epsilon_H = D_H^{+}\overrightarrow{\Delta\langle Q\rangle}_H$, and stochastic rates by *non-negative* least squares (stochastic rates must be $\geq 0$ for the error channel to be CPTP).

### Mid-circuit measurements as a circuit gadget

An ideal projective measurement is not invertible, so a noisy MCM cannot be written as "ideal measurement followed by $e^{\mathcal{L}}$".  Wysocki et al. sidestep this by representing a computational-basis MCM of qubit $q$ by a small circuit on $q$ plus one auxiliary *virtual* qubit $v$: prepare $v$ in $|0\rangle$, apply an ideal CNOT from $q$ to $v$, apply an error channel $e^{\mathcal{L}}$ on $(q, v)$, and finally read out $v$.  The instrument members for outcome $c$ are then

$$\mathcal{Q}_c[\rho] = (I\otimes\langle\langle c|)\; e^{\mathcal{L}}\; \mathrm{CNOT}_{q\to v}\;(\rho\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|).$$

Every ingredient of this gadget is a stabilizer operation.  So a circuit with $m$ MCMs on $n$ qubits is equivalent to an ordinary Clifford circuit on $n+m$ qubits in which each MCM has been replaced by a CNOT onto a fresh virtual qubit, and the virtual qubits are read out together with the data qubits at the end (the deferred-measurement principle).  The terminating measurement of this enlarged circuit produces the *joint* distribution of the final bitstring and the MCM outcomes, which is exactly what an experiment records.  Linearized GST therefore treats an MCM like any other gate: its error generators live on (measured qubit, virtual qubit), and $Z$-type observables on the enlarged system -- e.g. $Z_v$ (the MCM outcome) or $Z_q Z_v$ (the correlation between a later measurement and the MCM record) -- probe them.

The gadget representation is redundant: many two-qubit error generators produce the *same* first-order deviation of the instrument (e.g. $S_{IX}$ and $S_{IY}$ both flip the classical record), and some produce none at all (e.g. $H_{ZI}$, a $Z$ rotation of the measured qubit, commutes with the measurement).  This redundancy is called the *MCM gauge*.  The identifiable combinations are the FOMGI quantities; for Hamiltonian and Pauli-stochastic gadget errors there are 13 of them, and `pygsti.errorgenpropagation.mcmgadget.fomgi_ansatz` provides a gauge-free MCM ansatz with one representative generator per quantity:

| name | representative | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| `s_read` | $S_{IX}$ | pure readout (assignment) error: the record is flipped, the qubit is not |
| `s_meas` | $S_{XX}$ | bit flip *before* the measurement: record and post-measurement state are both flipped |
| `s_prep` | $S_{XI}$ | bit flip of the post-measurement state (record correct) |
| `r_meas_x`, `r_meas_y` | $H_{XX}$, $H_{YX}$ | the qubit is rotated about $x$ / $y$ just before it is measured (tilted measurement axis) |
| `r_prep_x_ind`, `r_prep_y_ind` | $H_{XI}$, $H_{YI}$ | outcome-independent rotation of the post-measurement state about $x$ / $y$ |
| `r_prep_x_dep`, `r_prep_y_dep` | $H_{XZ}$, $H_{YZ}$ | rotation of the post-measurement state whose direction depends on the outcome |
| `w0`, `w1`, `w2`, `w3` | $H_{ZY}$, $H_{IY}$, $H_{ZX}$, $H_{IX}$ | unitary "weakness": incomplete collapse, coherences that should be destroyed survive |

(Pauli strings list the measured qubit first and the virtual qubit second.)

## 2. The processor and the sparse error model

We use a 3-qubit processor with $X_{\pi/2}$ and $Y_{\pi/2}$ gates on each qubit, CZ gates between neighbouring qubits, and a mid-circuit measurement `Iz` on the middle qubit.

```{code-cell} ipython3
num_qubits = 3
edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2)]
pspec = QubitProcessorSpec(num_qubits, ['Gxpi2', 'Gypi2', 'Gcphase'], availability={'Gcphase': edges})
mcm_qubit = 1
```

The error model is a nested dictionary in the same format as the `lindblad_error_coeffs` argument of `create_cloud_crosstalk_model`.  Its keys are

* **gate keys** `(gate_name, qubit, ...)`, e.g. `('Gxpi2', 0)` or `('Gcphase', 0, 1)`;
* **MCM keys** `('Iz', qubit)`;
* the **SPAM keys** `'prep'` and `'povm'`.

Each value maps elementary error generators to rates.  An error generator is specified as `('H', pauli)` or `('S', pauli)`, where `pauli` is either a Pauli string on the operation's own qubits (`'X'` for a one-qubit gate, `'ZZ'` for a CZ) or an explicit-support string such as `'Z:2'` (a $Z$ error on qubit 2 -- this is how crosstalk is written).  For an MCM the plain form has two characters, `'<measured qubit><virtual qubit>'`, and the token `'v'` denotes the virtual qubit in the explicit form (e.g. `'XX:2,v'` for a correlated error on qubit 2 and the record).

Here we take a model in which every gate has coherent and stochastic errors that commute with its ideal action (a typical over/under-rotation plus dephasing along the rotation axis), single-qubit gates cause small coherent $Z$ rotations on their neighbours (pulse spillover), CZ gates have $ZZ$ and local $Z$ errors, SPAM has bit-flip errors, and the MCM has a 1% readout error together with some collapse and rotation errors.  These "true" rates are what we will try to recover.

```{code-cell} ipython3
rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)

def coherent():   # Hamiltonian rates in [-0.01, 0.01]  (rotation angles up to 0.02 rad)
    return rng.uniform(-0.01, 0.01)

def stochastic(): # Pauli-stochastic rates in [0, 0.002]
    return rng.uniform(0, 0.002)

def neighbours(q):
    return [q2 for (a, b) in edges for q2 in ((b,) if a == q else (a,) if b == q else ())]

error_model = {}
for q in pspec.qubit_labels:
    error_model[('Gxpi2', q)] = {('H', 'X'): coherent(), ('S', 'X'): stochastic(),
                                 **{('H', 'Z:%d' % q2): coherent() for q2 in neighbours(q)}}
    error_model[('Gypi2', q)] = {('H', 'Y'): coherent(), ('S', 'Y'): stochastic(),
                                 **{('H', 'Z:%d' % q2): coherent() for q2 in neighbours(q)}}
for (q1, q2) in edges:
    error_model[('Gcphase', q1, q2)] = {('H', 'ZZ'): coherent(), ('S', 'ZZ'): stochastic(),
                                        ('H', 'ZI'): coherent(), ('H', 'IZ'): coherent()}

fomgi_true = {'s_read': 0.01, 's_meas': 0.001, 's_prep': 0.002,
              'r_meas_y': 0.005, 'r_prep_x_ind': 0.01, 'w0': -0.005}
error_model[('Iz', mcm_qubit)] = mg.fomgi_ansatz(rates=fomgi_true)

error_model['prep'] = {('S', 'X:%d' % q): stochastic() for q in pspec.qubit_labels}
error_model['povm'] = {('S', 'X:%d' % q): 2 * stochastic() for q in pspec.qubit_labels}

error_model[('Gxpi2', 0)], dict(error_model[('Iz', mcm_qubit)])
```

`fomgi_ansatz` returns all 13 H+S FOMGI representatives; quantities we did not list have rate 0, but they are still *parameters of the ansatz* that will be estimated.

`build_model_parameter_indexing` enumerates the parameters of the ansatz.  It returns a `ModelParameterList` -- whose order defines the columns of every design matrix -- and the list of rates stored in the dictionary.  Each `ModelParameter` records the ansatz key it belongs to and its error generator as a Pauli string on the data qubits followed by one *virtual-qubit slot* (identity for everything except MCM parameters).

```{code-cell} ipython3
params, true_rates = lgst.build_model_parameter_indexing(error_model, qubit_labels=pspec.qubit_labels)
true_rates = np.array(true_rates)

print(len(params), "parameters for the keys", params.keys)
for p in params[:4] + [p for p in params if p.is_mcm][:3] + [p for p in params if p.is_spam][:2]:
    print("  ", p)
```

## 3. Circuits

Linearized GST needs circuits that are **shallow** (so that the first-order approximation is accurate -- the total error per circuit should be well below 1) but **varied** (so that every parameter affects some observable and the design matrix has full column rank).  Random circuits work well.  Below we sample random layers of gates -- leaving each qubit idle with some probability -- and insert between zero and two `Iz` measurements at random positions.

```{admonition} Diversity matters
:class: tip
If every layer contained a gate on *every* qubit, the crosstalk error "$Z$ on qubit 1 caused by the gate acting on qubit 0" could not be told apart from "$Z$ on qubit 1 caused by the gate acting on qubit 2" -- in every layer both would occur exactly once.  Layers with idle qubits break this degeneracy.  Section 4 shows how to check for this kind of problem.
```

```{code-cell} ipython3
def sample_circuit(rng, depth, num_mcms):
    layers = []
    for _ in range(depth):
        active = [q for q in pspec.qubit_labels if rng.random() < 0.75]
        components = []
        if rng.random() < 0.5:
            edge = edges[rng.integers(len(edges))]
            if all(q in active for q in edge):
                components.append(Label('Gcphase', edge))
                active = [q for q in active if q not in edge]
        components += [Label(str(rng.choice(['Gxpi2', 'Gypi2'])), q) for q in active]
        if components:
            layers.append(components[0] if len(components) == 1 else Label(components))
    for _ in range(num_mcms):
        layers.insert(rng.integers(0, len(layers) + 1), Label('Iz', mcm_qubit))
    return Circuit(layers, line_labels=pspec.qubit_labels)

num_circuits, depth = 300, 6
circuits = [sample_circuit(rng, depth, rng.integers(0, 3)) for _ in range(num_circuits)]

print("circuits with 0 / 1 / 2 mid-circuit measurements:", np.bincount([mg.num_mcms(c) for c in circuits]))

# an example circuit with a single MCM somewhere in the middle, used for illustration below
example = next(c for c in circuits if mg.num_mcms(c) == 1 and 2 <= mg.mcm_infos(c)[0].layer_index <= depth - 2)
print(example)
```

Internally, each MCM is replaced by a CNOT onto a new virtual qubit line (appended after the data qubits).  You never need to do this yourself, but it is instructive to look at the expanded circuit:

```{code-cell} ipython3
expanded, mcm_infos = mg.expand_mcm_circuit(example)
print(expanded)
print(mcm_infos)
```

A quick sanity check that every operation appearing in the circuits has an entry in the ansatz (an operation without one is silently assumed to be perfect):

```{code-cell} ipython3
lgst.unmodeled_gate_keys(circuits, params)
```

## 4. Design matrices

`create_design_matrix_list` computes one design matrix per circuit.  With `return_info=True` each entry is a `CircuitDesign` that also records the observables (one per row) and their ideal, error-free expectation values.  By default the observables are all $Z$-type Paulis of weight $\leq 2$ on the *expanded* system, i.e. on the data qubits and the virtual qubits holding the MCM records.

```{code-cell} ipython3
designs = lgst.create_design_matrix_list(circuits, params, return_info=True)

d = designs[circuits.index(example)]
print("design matrix shape (observables x parameters):", d.shape, " number of MCMs:", d.num_mcms)
print("observables:", [str(p) for p in d.paulis])
print("ideal expectation values:", d.ideal_expectations)
```

For this circuit the fourth qubit is the virtual qubit, so `+___Z` is the MCM outcome $\langle Z_v\rangle$ and `+_Z_Z` is its correlation with the final measurement of qubit 1.  Let us look at the block of the design matrix belonging to the MCM parameters:

```{code-cell} ipython3
mcm_columns = params.indices_for_key(('Iz', mcm_qubit))
fomgi_names = list(mg.fomgi_names_for_ansatz(error_model[('Iz', mcm_qubit)].keys()).values())
pd.DataFrame(d.design_matrix[:, mcm_columns], index=[str(p) for p in d.paulis], columns=fomgi_names)
```

Observables whose ideal value is $\pm1$ are first-order sensitive to stochastic errors only, and observables whose ideal value is $0$ to coherent errors only -- this is what allows the H and S sectors to be solved separately.  You can see it at work above: in this circuit the qubit is in a superposition when it is measured, so the MCM outcome is random ($\langle Z_v\rangle = 0$) but perfectly correlated with the final measurement of qubit 1 ($\langle Z_1 Z_v\rangle = 1$).  The pure readout error `s_read` and the post-measurement bit flip `s_prep` each reduce this correlation by $2s$, whereas `s_meas` -- a bit flip *before* the measurement, which flips the record together with the state -- leaves it intact.  The coherent tilt of the measurement axis `r_meas_x`, on the other hand, shifts the ideally vanishing expectation values $\langle Z_1\rangle$ and $\langle Z_v\rangle$.

Stacking all design matrices, we check that the full matrix has full column rank.  If it did not, `unidentifiable_directions` would list the combinations of parameters that the circuits cannot distinguish -- the remedy is usually to add circuits with a different structure.

```{code-cell} ipython3
D = np.vstack([d.design_matrix for d in designs])
print("stacked design matrix:", D.shape, " rank:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(D))
print("unidentifiable parameter combinations:", lgst.unidentifiable_directions(designs, params))
```

## 5. Simulating data

`rate_to_model` turns an ansatz (with rates) into a pyGSTi model: the gates and SPAM go into a `CloudNoiseModel`, and every MCM key becomes a noisy `Instrument` built from the gadget.  We use it to create the true noisy model, and an error-free reference model.

```{code-cell} ipython3
noisy_model = lgst.rate_to_model(pspec, error_model)
ideal_model = lgst.rate_to_model(pspec, error_model, rate_ests=np.zeros(len(params)))
```

The outcome labels of a circuit containing MCMs are tuples `(mcm outcome, ..., final bitstring)`; the data required by linearized GST are just the frequencies of these joint outcomes for every circuit.  We first compute the *exact* outcome probabilities (the $N\to\infty$ limit, which isolates the systematic error of the linear approximation) and then simulate a data set with 10,000 shots per circuit.

```{code-cell} ipython3
exact_probs = [noisy_model.probabilities(c) for c in circuits]

num_shots = 10000
dataset = pygsti.data.simulate_data(noisy_model, circuits, num_samples=num_shots, seed=2026)
frequencies = lgst.probability_dicts_from_dataset(dataset, circuits)

print(dataset[example])
```

(In an experiment you would run `circuits` on the device and load the counts into a `DataSet` with these outcome labels; nothing else changes.)

## 6. Estimating the error rates

`estimate_error_rates` takes the design matrices, the observed (and optionally the ideal) outcome distributions and the parameter list.  With `CircuitDesign` inputs the ideal expectation values are already known, so we pass `None` for them.  We start with the exact probabilities:

```{code-cell} ipython3
rates_exact, _ = lgst.estimate_error_rates(designs, [exact_probs, None], None, params)

types = np.array(params.errorgen_types())
for typ, name in [('H', 'Hamiltonian'), ('S', 'stochastic')]:
    mask = types == typ
    print("%-12s mean |true rate| = %.2e,  mean |estimate - true| = %.2e"
          % (name, np.abs(true_rates[mask]).mean(), np.abs(rates_exact - true_rates)[mask].mean()))
```

The residual estimation error of a few percent is the systematic error of the linear approximation (second-order effects of the errors in depth-6 circuits); it shrinks as the errors get smaller or the circuits shallower.

Now the finite-sample data, with a non-parametric bootstrap for the error bars (each circuit's outcome distribution is resampled with `num_shots` shots and the estimation is repeated):

```{code-cell} ipython3
rates_data, error_bars = lgst.estimate_error_rates(designs, [frequencies, None], None, params,
                                                   error_bars='bootstrap', error_bar_params=[30, num_shots], seed=1)

for typ, name in [('H', 'Hamiltonian'), ('S', 'stochastic')]:
    mask = types == typ
    print("%-12s mean |estimate - true| = %.2e,  mean error bar = %.2e"
          % (name, np.abs(rates_data - true_rates)[mask].mean(), error_bars[mask].mean()))
```

```{code-cell} ipython3
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 4.5), sharex=True, sharey=True)
for ax, (rates, errs, title) in zip(axes, [(rates_exact, None, 'no shot noise'),
                                            (rates_data, error_bars, '%d shots per circuit' % num_shots)]):
    for typ, color, label in [('H', 'tab:blue', 'Hamiltonian'), ('S', 'tab:orange', 'stochastic')]:
        mask = types == typ
        ax.errorbar(true_rates[mask], rates[mask], yerr=None if errs is None else errs[mask],
                    fmt='o', ms=4, color=color, label=label, alpha=0.8)
    lim = 1.15 * np.abs(true_rates).max()
    ax.plot([-lim, lim], [-lim, lim], 'k-', lw=0.8)
    ax.set_xlabel('true rate'); ax.set_title(title); ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
axes[0].set_ylabel('estimated rate'); axes[0].legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

The statistical uncertainty scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$ with the number of shots and roughly as $1/\sqrt{K}$ with the number of circuits, and linearized GST cannot amplify errors with deep circuits the way standard GST does -- so it typically needs more shots per circuit than GST to reach the same precision.

## 7. Interpreting the mid-circuit measurement

Because we used the FOMGI ansatz, the estimated MCM rates *are* the FOMGI quantities.  `mcm_fomgi_estimates` collects them by name (it also works for other gadget ansätze, in which case the rates are projected onto the FOMGI quantities):

```{code-cell} ipython3
fomgi_exact = lgst.mcm_fomgi_estimates(params, rates_exact)[('Iz', mcm_qubit)]
fomgi_data = lgst.mcm_fomgi_estimates(params, rates_data)[('Iz', mcm_qubit)]
fomgi_errs = error_bars[mcm_columns]

table = pd.DataFrame({'true': [fomgi_true.get(k, 0.0) for k in fomgi_exact],
                      'estimate (no shot noise)': list(fomgi_exact.values()),
                      'estimate (%d shots)' % num_shots: list(fomgi_data.values()),
                      'error bar': fomgi_errs}, index=list(fomgi_exact.keys()))
table.round(4)
```

```{code-cell} ipython3
x = np.arange(len(table))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 4))
ax.bar(x - 0.25, table['true'], 0.25, label='true', color='0.4')
ax.bar(x, table['estimate (no shot noise)'], 0.25, label='estimate, no shot noise', color='tab:green')
ax.bar(x + 0.25, table['estimate (%d shots)' % num_shots], 0.25, yerr=fomgi_errs, capsize=2,
       label='estimate, %d shots' % num_shots, color='tab:red')
ax.axhline(0, color='k', lw=0.8)
ax.set_xticks(x); ax.set_xticklabels(table.index, rotation=45, ha='right')
ax.set_ylabel('error rate'); ax.set_title('FOMGI quantities of the mid-circuit measurement on qubit %d' % mcm_qubit)
ax.legend(); ax.grid(axis='y', alpha=0.3)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

The dominant error is the $\approx 1\%$ pure readout error (`s_read`), followed by a coherent $x$ rotation of the post-measurement state (`r_prep_x_ind`), a tilt of the measurement axis towards $y$ (`r_meas_y`) and a small incomplete collapse (`w0`).  All of them are distinguished from each other by the data: a pure readout error only affects observables containing the MCM record, `s_prep` only affects the state after the measurement, and the coherent errors show up in circuits where the measured qubit is subsequently rotated.

### The MCM gauge

To see why the ansatz for the MCM is built from FOMGI representatives rather than from *all* two-qubit gadget error generators, look at the first-order map from gadget error generators to instrument deviations (the "crunch" map).  Of the 30 H+S generators on (measured qubit, virtual qubit), only 13 independent combinations are visible:

```{code-cell} ipython3
gadget_hs = mg.all_gadget_errorgen_keys(('H', 'S'))
crunch = mg.mcm_crunch_matrix(gadget_hs)
print("H+S gadget generators:", len(gadget_hs), "  rank of the crunch map:", np.linalg.matrix_rank(crunch, tol=1e-9))

invisible = [key for key, col in zip(gadget_hs, crunch.T) if np.linalg.norm(col) < 1e-9]
print("generators with no first-order effect on the instrument:", invisible)
print("S_IX and S_IY produce the same deviation:",
      np.allclose(crunch[:, gadget_hs.index(('S', 'IX'))], crunch[:, gadget_hs.index(('S', 'IY'))]))
```

Any gadget error model can be projected onto the FOMGI quantities with `fomgi_quantities`.  For instance, a readout error generated by $S_{IY}$ instead of $S_{IX}$, a measurement-axis tilt generated by $H_{YY}$ instead of $H_{XX}$, plus some gauge error generators:

```{code-cell} ipython3
alternative_mcm = {('S', 'IY'): 0.01, ('S', 'ZX'): 0.002, ('H', 'YY'): -0.005, ('H', 'IY'): 0.004,
                   ('H', 'ZI'): 0.03, ('S', 'IZ'): 0.01}   # the last two are pure MCM gauge
{k: round(v, 4) for k, v in mg.fomgi_quantities(alternative_mcm).items() if abs(v) > 1e-9}
```

If the device's MCM were described by this alternative model, fitting our FOMGI ansatz to its data would return these same quantities (up to the small systematic error of the linearization) -- the description is different but the physics, and hence the data, are the same:

```{code-cell} ipython3
alternative_model = dict(error_model)
alternative_model[('Iz', mcm_qubit)] = alternative_mcm
alternative_noisy_model = lgst.rate_to_model(pspec, alternative_model)
alt_probs = [alternative_noisy_model.probabilities(c) for c in circuits]
alt_rates, _ = lgst.estimate_error_rates(designs, [alt_probs, None], None, params)
{k: round(v, 4) for k, v in lgst.mcm_fomgi_estimates(params, alt_rates)[('Iz', mcm_qubit)].items() if abs(v) > 2e-4}
```

## 8. Using the estimated model

The estimated rates can be turned back into a pyGSTi model with `rate_to_model`, e.g. to predict the outcomes of other circuits.  Here we compare the predictions of the estimated model with the true model on a fresh set of circuits containing an MCM, using the total variation distance between the outcome distributions; the error-free model serves as a baseline.

```{code-cell} ipython3
estimated_model = lgst.rate_to_model(pspec, error_model, rate_ests=rates_data)
test_circuits = [sample_circuit(rng, depth, 1) for _ in range(40)]

def tvd(p, q):
    return 0.5 * sum(abs(p.get(k, 0.0) - q.get(k, 0.0)) for k in set(p) | set(q))

for name, model in [('error-free model', ideal_model), ('estimated model', estimated_model)]:
    tv = [tvd(noisy_model.probabilities(c), model.probabilities(c)) for c in test_circuits]
    print("%-18s mean TVD from the true outcome distributions: %.4f" % (name, np.mean(tv)))
```

`estimated_rates_dict` arranges the estimates in the same nested-dictionary format as the input ansatz (with explicit qubit supports), which is convenient for saving or for feeding them into other pyGSTi model constructors:

```{code-cell} ipython3

```

## 9. Practical notes

* **Validity of the linear approximation.** The systematic error grows with the total error per circuit (depth $\times$ typical rate).  Keep circuits shallow enough that the linear approximation holds; comparing the fits of two circuit depths is a simple diagnostic.  Linearized GST is also only as good as its ansatz -- errors not included in the model bias the estimates of the ones that are, although in practice the bias is modest when most of the error budget is modelled.
* **Identifiability.** Always check the rank of the stacked design matrix (`unidentifiable_directions`).  A genuine gauge freedom of the ansatz requires restricting the estimation to identifiable combinations; a rank deficiency caused by insufficiently varied circuits is fixed by adding circuits.
* **Observables.** `max_pauli_weight` (default 2) and `pauli_measurements` control which $Z$-type observables are used.  Higher-weight observables add information at no experimental cost but are noisier.
* **Mid-circuit measurements.** Only computational-basis (`Iz`) measurements are supported; a multi-qubit label such as `Label('Iz', (0, 1))` is treated as parallel single-qubit MCMs whose error models are given under `('Iz', 0)` and `('Iz', 1)`.  Outcome labels must use bitstrings for the MCM records.  When *simulating* models with MCMs, `rate_to_model` selects the matrix forward simulator, because pyGSTi's map simulator does not currently support instruments in implicit models.
* **Beyond H+S.** Pauli-correlation (`'C'`) and active (`'A'`) error generators are accepted in the ansatz and in the MCM gadget (`fomgi_ansatz(sectors='all')` gives all 28 FOMGI quantities); the H/S decoupling then no longer holds and a joint bounded least-squares problem is solved instead.
